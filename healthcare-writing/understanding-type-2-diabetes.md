Understanding Type 2 Diabetes: Symptoms, Risk Factors, and Management
Category: Healthcare Education
Content Type: Patient Education Article
Author: Maria Cervantes
Specialty: Healthcare and Medical Writing
Introduction
Type 2 diabetes is a long-term condition which involves the way that the blood glucose (blood sugar) is managed. Occurs when the body's cells lack the ability to respond to insulin or when the pancreas doesn't make enough insulin to keep blood glucose levels within a healthy range.
Diabetes is a chronic condition that impacts millions of people around the world, and can cause serious health issues if it is not properly managed. But, if people know they have it and take the necessary steps to treat it and practice a healthy lifestyle, they can manage it and maintain their quality of life.
Knowing the symptoms, risk factors and treatment options is crucial in developing awareness about diabetes.
What Happens in Type 2 Diabetes?
Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas which allows glucose to enter the cells to be used for energy.
People with type 2 diabetes are insulin resistant. With the passage of time, the pancreas may also become less efficient at making insulin to regulate blood sugar.
This results in a buildup of glucose in the blood and raises blood sugar.
Common Symptoms of Type 2 Diabetes
Symptoms may be gradual and some people may not experience any symptoms in the early stages.
Common symptoms include:
•	Unexplained weight loss.
•	Persistent fatigue.
•	Frequent urination.
•	Increased thirst.
•	Increased hunger.
•	Blurred vision.
•	Tingling or numbness in feet or hands.
•	Slow healing cuts and wounds.
•	Frequent infections.
If someone has these symptoms they should see their health care provider for proper evaluation and testing.
Risk Factors
Risk Factors
There are a number of things that make one more likely to develop type 2 diabetes.
1.	Family History
There is a higher risk for type 2 diabetes if you have a parent or sibling who has the disease.
2.	Age
The risk is greater in older people, especially those over age 45. But, adults, teens and children can also develop Type 2 diabetes.
3.	Physical Inactivity
Inactivity can lead to insulin resistance and help bring about type 2 diabetes.
4.	Excess Body Weight
Overweight and obesity are risk factors, but can occur at any weight.
5.	The history of Gestational Diabetes
Women who had diabetes while pregnant are more likely to get type 2 diabetes later in life.
The knowledge of these risk factors can assist people to identify when screening and preventative interventions might be useful.
How Is Type 2 Diabetes Diagnosed?
Blood tests to measure glucose levels are used to diagnose type 2 diabetes.
Tests that are typically performed include:
1. A1C Test
This test is used to determine the average level of blood sugar for about three months.
•	Normal: Below 5.7%.
•	Prediabetes: 5.7%–6.4%.
•	Diabetes: 6.5% or higher.
2. Fasting Plasma Glucose Test
This test is done to determine the amount of blood glucose present after not eating for 8 hours.
•	Normal: ≤ 99 mg/dL.
•	Prediabetes: 100–125 mg/dL.
•	Diabetes: >=126 mg/dL.
3. Oral Glucose Tolerance Test
This test determines the body's response to glucose following an oral intake of a specific glucose beverage.
If the blood glucose level is 200 mg/dL or above, it is in the diabetes range.
If there is no definite hyperglycemia diagnosis, an abnormal diagnostic result is a typical indicator that the result needs to be repeated or another diagnostic test should be performed.
Managing Type 2 Diabetes
Although type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition, appropriate management can help prevent or delay complications.
Type 2 Diabetes is a chronic disease, but with proper management, the disease and its complications can be avoided or postponed.
1. Follow a Balanced Eating Plan
A well-planned dietary program may help to control blood sugars.
Consider:
•	Consuming a diverse diet of veggies and fruits.
•	Eating whole grains and high fiber foods.
•	To include the correct lean protein sources.
•	Monitoring carbohydrate portions.
•	Limiting sugar-sweetened beverages.
•	Working with a registered dietitian whenever possible.
Diabetics don't have to cut out all carbs and their favorite treats. Plan each individual meal, it is more sustainable.
2. Engage in Regular Physical Activity
Exercise increases the body's efficiency with insulin and may help control blood sugar levels.
To get at least 150 minutes of moderate level aerobic exercise done a week, many adults should do so according to their health and abilities.
Walking, swimming, cycling and other appropriate activities may be beneficial.
3. Take Prescribed Medication
Some people can control their blood glucose levels with lifestyle changes, others need medication.
Medications may be used to treat it, such as oral medications, injectable medications or insulin.
Depending on the people's health status, blood glucose levels, other health conditions and clinical recommendations, the correct treatment is given.
It is recommended that the patients use the medicines as directed and ask their doctors' advice before changing the medicines.
4. Monitor Blood Glucose Levels
By monitoring regularly, people can learn about their blood glucose control and how their food, activity, stress and medicine levels impact their blood glucose.
Monitoring may be done more or less often, depending on the treatment program of the person.
5. Attend Regular Healthcare Appointments
Routine appointments help healthcare professionals monitor diabetes management and identify potential complications.
Important assessments may include:
•	A1C testing.
•	Blood pressure monitoring.
•	Cholesterol testing.
•	Kidney function assessment.
•	Eye examinations.
•	Foot examinations.
Regular check-ups enable medical professionals to keep an eye on the management of diabetes and detect possible complications.
Some evaluations that are important include:
•	Cholesterol testing.
•	A1C testing.
•	Eye examinations.
•	Blood pressure monitoring.
•	Kidney function assessment.
•	Foot examinations.
Potential Complications
With time, poorly managed diabetes can cause blood vessels and nerves to become damaged.
Potential complications include:
•	Cardiovascular disease.
•	Chronic kidney disease.
•	Stroke.
•	Peripheral neuropathy.
•	Diabetic retinopathy and vision loss.
•	Foot ulcers and infections.
The risk of these complications can be decreased with early intervention and continued management.
Can Type 2 Diabetes Be Prevented?
Type 2 diabetes may sometimes be prevented or delayed, especially for people who are at risk due to prediabetes or other risk factors.
There are helpful preventative strategies available such as:
•	Maintain a normal weight.
•	Regular exercise.
•	Choosing nutritious meals.
•	Controlling over-consumption of sugary drinks.
•	Going to suggested health assessments.
Structured lifestyle intervention programs can help decrease the risk of developing type 2 diabetes in individuals at risk due to prediabetes.
Conclusion
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition that can be managed, but needs regular treatment and care.
Regular monitoring, using a treatment plan specific to the person, and early signs and symptom recognition can help people manage blood glucose better and decrease complications.
Educating patients about diabetes is still an essential component of diabetes care since educated people are better prepared to engage in health care decisions.
