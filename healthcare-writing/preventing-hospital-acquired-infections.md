The Role of Nurses in Preventing Hospital-Acquired Infections: Essential Practices for Patient Safety
Category: Nursing and Patient Safety
Content Type: Healthcare Professional Article
Author: Maria Cervantes, RN
Specialty: Nursing Practice and Infection Prevention
Introduction
Healthcare patient safety is among the most crucial duties in the healthcare sector. Nurses provide direct care to patients, conduct clinical procedures, prescribe drugs and help patients with activities of daily living each day. In the health care setting, however, microorganisms can be present that can lead to infections.
Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are infections patients can get when they receive health care that they do not have or have an early sign of when they first received care. These infections may lead to more treatment, longer hospital stays, higher health care costs and complications.
Through regular infection prevention practices, patient education and adherence to the current clinical guidelines, nurses have a vital role in preventing the spread of infectious organisms.
Understanding Healthcare-Associated Infections
HAIs can occur in many types of health care settings, such as hospitals, outpatient facilities or long-term care facilities.
Examples include:
•	Catheter associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs).
•	Central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSIs).
•	Surgical site infections (SSIs).
•	Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP).
•	Infections that develop because of multidrug-resistant organisms.
The infection can develop if the germs enter the body through a medical device, a surgical wound, the respiratory tract or other means.
Working together is key to an effective infection prevention response by nurses, doctors, infection prevention specialists, cleaning staff, patients and health systems.
The Role of Nurses in Infection Prevention
1. Maintaining Proper Hand Hygiene
Hand hygiene is one of the critical aspects to minimise the spread of infectious organisms in a healthcare environment.
Nurses are often transporting items or moving from patient to equipment and clinical tasks. Patients, equipment and clinical activity often moves from nurse to nurse. Organisms can be spread by direct contact or contaminated surfaces if hand hygiene is not performed.
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend that healthcare workers wash their hands:
•	Before carrying out an aseptic procedure.
•	Prior to transferring from a contaminated site to clean site in the same individual.
•	Once the healthcare worker has touched the patient.
•	Following contact with blood or body fluids or contaminated surfaces.
•	Prior to contact with a patient.
•	Following contact with patient's immediate area.
•	Immediately after removing gloves.
Alcohol-based hand sanitizers are usually recommended most of the time when hands are not visibly soiled. If hands are visibly dirty, use soap and water.
Hand hygiene helps to protect patients and healthcare personnel.
2. Using Personal Protective Equipment Correctly
Personal protective equipment (PPE) is used to minimize exposure to potentially infectious materials when providing care to patients.
PPE would be worn and could include:
•	Gloves.
•	Gowns.
•	Surgical masks.
•	Respirators.
•	Eye Protection/Face Shades.
Risk assessment before each clinical activity, and choosing protective equipment will be the responsibility of the nurse.
The correct technique when removing gloves is also essential as improper removal may lead to contamination of the hands, clothing or environment.
More importantly, hand hygiene needs to be done and does not take the place of gloves. Hand Hygiene should be done in advance of wearing gloves and after removing gloves from the patient.
3. Maintaining Aseptic Technique
Aseptic procedure is important when using procedures that require invasive medical devices, wounds or other places where microorganisms can enter the body.
Examples include:
•	Inserting and keeping urinary catheters in place.
•	Performing wound care.
•	Handling IV access devices.
•	Administering injections.
•	Operating invasive medical devices.
Nurses must use clinical protocols, use proper aseptic technique and not introduce unnecessary contamination of sterile equipment and supplies.
The practices are meant for minimising the risk of infectious organisms entering into susceptible parts of the body.
4. Proper Care and Maintenance of Medical Devices
When these medical devices aren't used and maintained appropriately, they can contribute to an infection risk, like for urinary and central venous catheters, or ventilators.
Nurses play a role in preventing infection by:
•	Frequent checking of insertion sites for infection.
•	Sustaining suitable device cleanliness.
•	Adhering to maintenance procedures, as may be required for the facilities.
•	Determining the continued clinical need.
•	Providing information on possible complications.
•	Timely removal: supporting timely removal when a device is no longer required and removal is clinically appropriate.
Standard assessment and device management protocol minimise un-necessary exposure to infection.
5. Environmental Cleaning and Disinfection
Microorganisms can be spread by contaminated surfaces and equipment that is not processed properly.
Nurses are responsible for following the hospital policies for cleaning and disinfection, especially for those items that are commonly used and touched by patients.
Important practices include:
•	Washing patient care equipment (except disposable) between patients as needed.
•	Cleaning common equipment per manufacturer's directions and facility policies.
•	Safely handling contaminated equipment.
•	Reporting of blood or other potentially infectious materials spills.
•	Cleanliness of patient-care areas.
Nurses should also talk to environmental services staff if extra cleaning/disinfection is needed.
6. Following Isolation and Transmission-Based Precautions
Patients may need extra infection prevention precautions if they have a suspected or confirmed infection that can be transmitted via contact, droplets or air.
Nurses need to be aware and adhere to the proper transmission-based precautions set by the health care facility.
These may involve:
•	Contact precautions.
•	Droplet precautions.
•	Airborne precautions.
The right placement of patients, protection, signs and health care staff communication minimize transmission opportunities.
Precautions should be chosen based on suspected and/or confirmed infection and current clinical guidance.
7. Educating Patients and Their Families
Patient education is also vital to infection prevention.
Educating patients and families about the transmission of infections and what they can do to minimize exposure can help nurses.
Education may include:
•	Using the correct handwashing techniques.
•	Maintaining cleanliness of wounds and adhering to wound care guidelines.
•	Knowing the significance of the prescribed treatments.
•	Following isolation instructions.
•	Recognizing possible signs of infection.
•	Prevention of superfluous contact with intrusive medical equipment.
Infection prevention is a shared responsibility with patient and families who are encouraged to participate.
Recognizing and Reporting Possible Signs of Infection
Timely clinical assessment and intervention requires early recognition.
Patients should be watched for signs of infection such as:
•	Fever or abnormally low body temperature.
•	Redness, swelling or warmth around wounds or insertion sites.
•	Increased or new pain.
•	Purulent drainage.
•	Any alteration in breathing pattern.
•	New confusion or changes in mental status, especially vulnerable patients.
•	Changes in pertinent laboratory parameters.
These are not infection specific and should be considered in the light of the patient's clinical condition.
If there is any suspicion of infection, nurses should immediately inform the relevant health care worker and adopt the assessment, documentation and escalation procedures laid out.
Challenges in Infection Prevention
Even though there are guidelines in place, health-care workers may face problems that impact infection prevention.
Common challenges include:
1.	The level of patient work and staffing pressures.
2.	Poor access to hand hygiene and/or PPE.
3.	Uneven compliance of infection control measures.
4.	Limited opportunities for continued staff learning.
5.	Improper handling of medical equipment and/or maintenance.
These challenges must be tackled by training staff, ensuring they have adequate supplies, providing supportive leadership, monitoring for infections and conducting continuous quality improvement.
The Importance of Evidence-Based Nursing Practice
Infection prevention practices should be based on the most up to date evidence, professional standards and policies for the care facilities.
Nurses should engage in continuous education, stay up to date with new guidelines on infection prevention, and be involved in quality improvement programs.
Infection surveillance data and compliance monitoring can also help pinpoint areas for improvement of practice and assess the success of prevention strategies on healthcare teams.
To achieve a strong culture of patient safety, it is important that accountability, team work and consistent implementation of infection prevention strategies is in place.
Conclusion
The prevention of HAI is a multi-disciplinary responsibility, but nurses are at a particular risk of infection due to their close and regular contact with patients.
Nurses help limit opportunities for infection to be passed on through proper hand hygiene, appropriate use of PPE, use of aseptic technique, management of medical devices, environmental cleaning and patient education.
The use of evidence-based infection prevention measures helps keep patients safer, leads to improved clinical care and helps keep healthcare environments safer.
